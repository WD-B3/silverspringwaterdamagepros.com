Saving Wet Photos and Documents After Water Damage in Silver Spring

Handling, rinsing, freezing and drying, and when to hand irreplaceable items to a specialist.

By the Silver Spring Water Damage Pros team · Published November 10, 2026

Furniture can be replaced. Photographs, wedding certificates, deeds and children's drawings cannot. When water reaches paper, the clock is short, because wet paper sticks together, ink runs and growth can start within a few days.

These steps buy time. They are general guidance for clean water. For anything valuable or irreplaceable, or anything touched by sewage or floodwater, a conservator or a document recovery service is the safer choice.

First, make sure it is safe

Do not step into a room with water near electrical equipment. Wear gloves if the water might be dirty. Items touched by sewage or floodwater should not be handled by hand, and many are not worth the risk.

Handle as little as possible

Wet paper tears easily. Lift items by the edges, support them from below and avoid peeling stuck sheets apart. A stack of wet photographs that has stuck together should be left stuck and frozen, then separated later with care.

Photographs

  1. Keep them wet or freeze them. Dried photos stick to surfaces and to each other.
  2. If you can, rinse gently in clean, cool water to remove mud. Do not rub the surface.
  3. Lay them face up on a clean, absorbent surface such as paper towels or blotting paper.
  4. Change the paper as it soaks up water, and keep them out of direct sun.
  5. Do not use a hair dryer or a heat source.

Books and loose papers

For a damp book, stand it on its tail edge with the pages gently fanned. For a soaked book, place sheets of paper towel or wax paper between pages every so often and let it dry flat. Replace the sheets as they get wet. Large amounts can be frozen to buy time.

Loose papers can be laid flat to dry on a clean surface. Weight the corners lightly to reduce curling.

Why freezing helps

Freezing stops growth and prevents more damage while you decide what to do. Seal items in freezer bags with wax paper between layers where possible. A conservator or a document recovery company can then use controlled drying.

Important records to rescue first

  • Birth, marriage and death certificates
  • Property deeds and insurance policies
  • Tax and financial records
  • Medical records and prescriptions
  • Family photographs and irreplaceable keepsakes

Digital copies after drying

Once an item is dry, scan or photograph it. A digital copy protects what remains. Store it somewhere that does not share a flood zone with the original, such as cloud storage or a drive kept elsewhere.

Dry the room too

Documents stored in a wet room will get wet again if the room does not dry. A restoration professional uses structural drying to bring the space back to normal. If the loss came from a basement, see basement water damage cleanup.

Mold and odor on paper

Paper that stays damp can grow spots in a few days and carry a musty smell. If you see spotting, isolate the item in a bag and ask a conservator for advice. Do not brush off spots, since that can spread them and damage the paper.

Storing papers so they survive the next leak

  • Use lidded plastic bins instead of cardboard boxes
  • Keep bins on shelves, not on the floor
  • Store originals of key records in a place that does not share risk with your basement
  • Scan important papers once a year
  • Label bins so you can find what to rescue first

Fire safes and waterproof boxes

A fire safe is not always a water safe. Many are not sealed, and water can get in. If you keep records in a safe, check whether it is rated for water. A waterproof box or a bag with a sealed zipper is a low-cost upgrade for the few documents you cannot replace.

Getting help with important records

Replacing a lost birth certificate, deed or tax record takes time. Start by listing what you have, then contact the issuing office for copies of anything damaged. Keep a record of requests, since some offices take weeks.

A simple triage list

  1. Identify the five items you would hate to lose.
  2. Freeze or air dry those first.
  3. Sort the rest by how easily replaced they are.
  4. Photograph anything you give up on.

Having a short list in mind helps you act quickly when everything feels urgent at once.

Maps, art and large formats

Large prints, maps and artwork need flat, supported drying. Lay them on clean blotting paper and avoid hanging them wet. Framed pieces should be removed from their frames only by someone trained, because the glass and backing may stick to the surface. A conservator can advise on a valuable piece before you touch it.

Digital media

Discs, memory cards and hard drives can sometimes be recovered even after water. Do not plug them in. Rinse off mud with clean water if needed, let them dry and take them to a data recovery service. The sooner you act, the better the odds.

A note for Silver Spring and Lancaster County families

Lancaster County has a deep local history, and many families keep records, photos and heirlooms in basements or attics. Columbia's historic collections include the National Watch and Clock Museum on Poplar Street, a reminder that careful storage of valuable things matters.

If you live in Columbia or Mount Joy and store records low in the house, move them to a higher shelf in a plastic bin with a lid. See flood damage cleanup if water has already reached a lower level.

Questions Silver Spring homeowners ask

Can I use a hair dryer on wet photos?

No. Heat can curl the paper and fuse emulsion to surfaces. Air dry or freeze.

Do I have to freeze everything?

Only what you cannot dry in a day or two. Freezing buys time for valuable items.

Who restores very valuable items?

A conservator or a document recovery service. Ask your insurer whether the cost is covered.
